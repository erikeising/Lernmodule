The operator is your instruction — not a suggestion

One operator = one cognitive operation. If an exam task says analyse, you do not summarise. If it says outline, you do not give your opinion. The operator tells you the type of thinking required — misreading it costs you the entire task mark. When in doubt: check the AFB level of the operator and match your response accordingly.

What is it?

AFB I — Requirement Level 1

A summary (or outline) reproduces the main ideas of a source text accurately, concisely, and in your own words. You show what the text says — not how it says it, and not what you think about it. The finished text is at least two thirds shorter than the original.

Outline vs. Summarise: Both are AFB I. An outline typically calls for a more structured, point-based presentation of content (often bullet-style or numbered). A summary is usually written in continuous prose. Both forbid personal opinion and analysis.

Structure

Intro
Sentence
One sentence only. Name the author, title, text type, place and date of publication, and the main idea. Example: "In her 2024 article 'Ambition Redefined' in The Guardian, journalist Maya Cole argues that young Americans are redefining success beyond financial achievement."
Main
Points
Chronological or logical order. Work through the text from beginning to end. Paraphrase — do not copy. Omit details, examples, and rhetoric. Include only what is essential to the argument or narrative.
No
Conclusion
Summaries do not need a separate conclusion. The last main point is usually sufficient. Do not add your opinion or evaluation — that belongs in a Comment (AFB III).

Do's and Don'ts

✓ DO
  • Use the present tense throughout
  • Use your own words — rephrase actively
  • Include an introductory sentence with all key metadata
  • Use connectives (furthermore, however, as a result…)
  • Use formal language — no contractions
  • Aim for ≥ ⅔ shorter than the original
✗ DON'T
  • Quote from the text
  • Give your own opinion
  • Analyse the author's style or language
  • Create suspense or dramatise
  • Use direct speech
  • Include irrelevant details
  • Copy the original's sentence structure

What is it?

AFB II — Requirement Level 2

An analysis of a non-fictional text (article, speech, essay, interview) examines how the author constructs their argument: language choices, rhetorical strategies, stylistic devices, and structure — and explains the effect each creates on the reader. You do not summarise. You do not give your opinion. You interpret evidence.

Structure

Introduction
Context + central claim (thesis). Name the text and establish context briefly. Then state your central claim: what is the text's main intention/effect, and how does it achieve this? The last sentence of the intro should be your thesis.
Main Part
One paragraph per aspect. For each aspect (e.g. word choice, rhetorical devices, structure, tone): identify → quote selectively → explain function and effect on reader. Choose your evidence carefully — quality over quantity.
Conclusion
Abstract level — more than just a summary. Refer back to the task and your thesis. State what your findings reveal on a higher level: What does this analysis tell us about the author's overall strategy or intention? Do not introduce new evidence here.

What to analyse — key aspects

Language & Style
  • Word choice (formal/informal, emotive, connotations)
  • Sentence length and rhythm
  • Rhetorical devices (repetition, rhetorical questions, tricolon…)
  • Tone (ironic, urgent, empathetic…)
  • Use of statistics, quotes, anecdotes
Structure & Argumentation
  • How the argument develops (chronological, problem-solution, compare-contrast)
  • Paragraph transitions and cohesion
  • How the introduction creates engagement
  • How the conclusion reinforces the thesis
  • Target audience and intended effect
Always go P → E → E: Point (what you observe) → Evidence (selective quote + line number) → Effect (what this does to the reader and why). An observation without an effect explanation is incomplete analysis.

The borderline operator: assess / evaluate

AFB II → III — Borderline operator

Assess (or evaluate) asks you to consider a view, claim, or argument in a balanced way — weighing points for and against — and then reach a reasoned judgement. It sits at the boundary between AFB II and III: the analysis of evidence is AFB II; the final evaluative judgement is AFB III.


Example task: "Assess the author's view of the present impact of the American Dream."


What this requires: examine what the author claims → consider evidence that supports it → consider evidence or arguments that qualify or challenge it → reach an overall evaluative judgement. Do not simply summarise both sides — that stays in AFB I/II. The assessment is your concluded position, substantiated by the evidence you have weighed.

Assess vs. Analyse: Analyse asks how something works. Assess asks how valid, effective, or convincing it is. An analysis explains; an assessment judges. In practice, a strong assess response contains analysis as its foundation — but the conclusion must be explicitly evaluative.

What is it?

AFB II — Requirement Level 2

A characterisation analyses a fictional character systematically using textual evidence. You describe who the character is, what they do, how they relate to others, and what their character traits are — and you support every claim with a quote or line reference from the text. This is not a retelling. Every statement must be provable from the text.

Structure

Introduction
Who + focus statement. Name the character, give basic context (text, author, situation), and state the focus of your characterisation. What is the most important thing about this character that your analysis will demonstrate?
Main Part
Organised by aspect — not retelling. Group your findings by category: outward appearance → background/life → behaviour → relationships → character traits → mood/feelings. Each paragraph = one aspect. Every claim = one piece of evidence from the text (quote + line number).
Conclusion
Essence — not summary. What is the character's fundamental significance in the text? How does the characterisation you have outlined contribute to the text's themes or effect on the reader?

What to include

Content aspects to cover
  • Outward appearance (if described)
  • Background and life situation
  • Family and social context
  • Behaviour (what they do, how they react)
  • Relationships with other characters
  • At least two character traits — named and evidenced
  • Mood and feelings in the current situation
✗ Common mistakes
  • Retelling the plot instead of characterising
  • Stating character traits without textual evidence
  • Claiming things the text does not support
  • Forgetting line numbers on quotes
  • Mixing up characterisation and analysis of narrative perspective
  • Writing in the past tense

What is it?

AFB III — Linear argumentation

A comment is a linear, one-directional argument: you state a clear position and defend it with at least three strong arguments, each supported by evidence. Counter-arguments are acknowledged and refuted — but your position does not waver. The reader should finish knowing exactly where you stand and why.

Comment vs. Discussion — the essential difference: A comment argues one position from start to finish (linear). A discussion deliberately weighs opposing sides (dialectical: 2 PRO, 2 CONTRA). They are never combined in one exam task — it is always either/or. Reading the operator carefully is non-negotiable.

Structure

Introduction
Problem + thesis statement. Present the issue. The last sentence must state your position clearly and specifically: not "This is a complex issue" but "I argue that…" or "It is my contention that…"
Argument 1
Weakest → strongest (build up). State your first argument. Support it with evidence (facts, statistics, quotes, examples). If a counter-argument exists, address and refute it here.
Argument 2
Develop your case further. Each paragraph = one line of argument. Transition explicitly. Build towards your most compelling point.
Argument 3
(minimum)
Your strongest argument — save it for here. The most persuasive, best-evidenced argument lands last. This is what the reader remembers when they reach your conclusion.
Conclusion
Restate thesis + broader significance. Do not just summarise. Restate your position in different words and point to broader implications: What does this mean for society, for the future, for the reader?

Strong vs. weak arguments — know the difference

✓ Strong arguments have…
  • A clear claim — one idea per paragraph, stated in the first sentence
  • Concrete evidence — statistics, real examples, quotes, or established facts (not "many people think…")
  • Explanation — you connect the evidence to your claim explicitly
  • Relevance — the argument directly supports your thesis, not a related topic
  • Counter-argument awareness — you anticipate the objection and address it
✗ Weak arguments…
  • Make a claim without any evidence ("It is obvious that…")
  • Use vague generalisations ("Everyone knows…", "Most people feel…")
  • Repeat the thesis in different words without adding substance
  • Rely on personal anecdote as if it were universal truth
  • Are logically circular ("X is bad because X has bad effects")
  • Ignore or dismiss counter-arguments without engaging them
The evidence test: For every argument, ask: "What would someone say against this — and how do I know my evidence is stronger than theirs?" If you cannot answer that, the argument needs more work before it belongs in a comment.

Defining terms — the technique

When and how to define

Define a term when it is central to your argument and its meaning is contestable. Use this formula: [Term] refers to [category] + [distinguishing features].


Example: "The term 'American Dream' refers to the belief — central to American national identity — that individual effort and merit guarantee upward social and economic mobility, regardless of one's origins."


Do not define terms that are obvious or uncontested. One well-placed definition per text is usually sufficient.

What is it?

AFB III — Dialectical argumentation

A discussion is a dialectical text: you deliberately present and weigh both sides of a question — exactly 2 PRO and 2 CONTRA arguments — before arriving at a reasoned conclusion. Unlike a comment, you do not take sides until the conclusion. The structure mirrors a genuine intellectual debate: you give the strongest possible case for both positions.

The either/or principle: An exam task will say either comment on or discuss — never both. If the operator is discuss, presenting only one side is a structural error, regardless of how strong your arguments are. If the operator is comment on, writing a balanced discussion misses the task entirely.

Structure

Introduction
Present the question — do not take sides yet. Introduce the topic and the central question or statement to be discussed. Explain why this question is relevant or contested. End with a neutral framing sentence that signals you will weigh both sides.
PRO 1
First argument in favour. State clearly. Support with concrete evidence. Present this argument fairly — as if you believe it.
PRO 2
Second argument in favour. A different line of argument from PRO 1 — not a repetition. Each PRO paragraph = one distinct argument.
CONTRA 1
First argument against. Transition explicitly: "On the other hand, …" / "However, critics argue that…". Support with evidence. Give this argument its full weight.
CONTRA 2
Second argument against. A distinct argument from CONTRA 1. By the end of this paragraph, both sides have been genuinely represented.
Conclusion
Your reasoned judgement — now take sides. Having weighed both sides, state which you find more convincing and why. This is the only place in a discussion where your own position is required. Justify it with reference to the strongest argument from your analysis.

Strong vs. weak arguments — know the difference

✓ Strong arguments have…
  • A clear claim — one idea per paragraph, stated in the first sentence
  • Concrete evidence — statistics, real examples, quotes, or established facts (not "many people think…")
  • Explanation — you connect evidence to claim explicitly
  • Relevance — directly addresses the discussion question
  • Specificity — names figures, places, policies, texts where possible
✗ Weak arguments…
  • Make claims without evidence ("It is obvious that…")
  • Use vague generalisations ("Most people feel…", "Everyone agrees…")
  • Are one-sided in a task that requires balance
  • Mirror each other across PRO/CONTRA without real distinction
  • Rely on personal anecdote as universal evidence
  • Are logically circular ("X is bad because X causes bad outcomes")
Symmetry test: If you replaced your CONTRA arguments with your PRO arguments, would anyone notice? If yes — your PRO and CONTRA are too similar. Each of the four arguments must be genuinely distinct in its claim, evidence, and line of reasoning.
